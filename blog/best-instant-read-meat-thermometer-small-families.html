How I Use It in Foodiano World (The Desi Twist)

On my YouTube channel Foodiano World, the recipes where a thermometer changes everything are the desi ones — and this is where the desi twist comes in. The twist I love: desi cooking is full of dishes where the outside lies to you. A seekh kebab browns fast on a hot tawa while the centre is still cool; a chicken karahi looks ready when the masala darkens, but the thigh meat needs to reach 165°F to be safe and juicy; a whole roast chicken (the kind served at a family dawat) can look golden while the thigh is undercooked. For a small family cooking desi food daily, buyers say that one habit — probe the thickest piece, trust the number, not the colour — is what turns good cooks into consistent ones.

FAQ

Is an instant-read meat thermometer worth it for a small family?

Buyers say yes, because guessing is how chicken dries out and kebabs stay undercooked in the middle. A small family cooks the same few meats again and again, so one quick check — chicken to 165°F, ground meat to 160°F, steaks to 145°F plus a short rest — takes the guesswork out of every dinner. Buyers say a thermometer under $15 that lives in a drawer next to the stove gets used far more than a big gadget that needs charging or an app.

How do I check chicken or kebab temperature correctly?

Buyers say to push the probe into the thickest part of the meat, away from bone, fat and the pan, and wait for the number to settle. For a whole chicken, check the thickest part of the thigh; for seekh kebabs and burgers, check the centre of the thickest piece. Buyers say to wash the probe after raw meat and before cooked food, and to check more than one piece when cooking a batch, since pieces cook at different speeds.

Buyers say the TP19H carries an IP65 waterproof rating, so it can be washed under running tap water after cooking, and its display rotates automatically so it stays readable in either hand. On speed, buyers say readings feel near-instant in everyday use, while one independent test measured 2.5 to 5 seconds in a lab-style test — still fast enough for a weeknight dinner, but worth knowing if you expected a literal one-second reading every time.
