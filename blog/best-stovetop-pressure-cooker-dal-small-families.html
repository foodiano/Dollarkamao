What to Look For

The right size — 3 litres for a small family. A 3-litre cooker feeds 3 to 4 people with dal for one meal. Bigger cookers waste gas and counter space for a small family; smaller ones cannot hold enough dal and water. Three litres is the size buyers keep recommending.

An inner-fitting lid. The lid should fit inside the body and lock so it cannot open while pressure is inside. This is the classic Hawkins design, and it is the single most important safety feature on a stovetop cooker.

A pressure regulator that handles frothy food. Dal foams and sputters. A good regulator keeps pressure in a narrow, steady range and reduces that sputtering — the Hawkins Classic's regulator was redesigned exactly for this, and buyers say it keeps dal inside the pot instead of on the stove.

Fast, even heating. Aluminium heats up quickly and evenly, which matters when you want dal done in 15 minutes on a weeknight. Stainless steel heats slower but resists acidic foods better. For daily dal, fast heating wins.

Proven reviews (4+ stars, huge volume). The Hawkins 3-litre family of cookers carries a 4.3 to 4.5-star rating across thousands of buyers, and the Classic is the default "best overall" pick in 2026 roundups for Indian cooking. That volume is the best quality check a research-based review can offer.

Works on your stove. The aluminium Classic works on gas stoves — the stove most desi homes cook dal on. If you cook on induction, pick the stainless steel Hawkins instead, which is induction-ready.

How I Use It in Foodiano World (The Desi Twist)

On my YouTube channel Foodiano World, dal is the dish that never leaves the weekly menu — and the pressure cooker is what makes it possible on a busy night. My timings, counted in whistles after the cooker reaches pressure: moong dal and masoor dal take 2 whistles, arhar (toor) dal takes 3, and soaked chana dal takes 4 to 5. One cup of dal to about two and a half cups of water, a pinch of turmeric and salt, and the dal is soft enough to mash with a spoon.

The tadka goes in the same pot: open the lid, heat a spoon of ghee, add cumin seeds, garlic and dried red chilli, and pour it sizzling over the dal. One pot, one wash. A few rules I follow every time: never fill the cooker more than two-thirds full with dal, always let the pressure fall on its own before opening, and wash the gasket and the vent pipe after every use so the next dal cooks clean. FAQ

Is a stovetop pressure cooker safe for cooking dal?

Yes, when it is used the way the maker instructs. The Hawkins Classic has an inner-fitting lid that cannot open while pressure is inside, a pressure regulator that controls steam in a narrow range, and a safety valve under the lid handle that deflects steam downwards. Never fill a pressure cooker more than two-thirds full with dal — frothy dal needs headroom — and always let the pressure fall naturally before opening the lid.

What size pressure cooker is best for a small family making dal?

A 3-litre cooker is the sweet spot for a family of 3 to 4. It cooks enough dal for one meal plus a little leftover, without a huge heavy pot to wash. A 5-litre cooker makes sense only if you batch-cook dal for the whole week or regularly feed 5 or more people. For most small families, 3 litres is the size buyers recommend again and again.

Aluminium or stainless steel — which pressure cooker is better for dal?

Both work well, and the choice is mostly about budget and weight. Aluminium heats up fast, costs less, and is lighter to lift — which is why the Hawkins Classic aluminium cooker is the best-selling dal cooker in desi kitchens. Stainless steel costs more, weighs more, and resists acidic foods like tomatoes and tamarind a little better. If you cook dal daily and want the cheapest proven option, aluminium wins. If you cook a lot of tomato-heavy dishes and want the longest-lasting body, pick stainless steel.
